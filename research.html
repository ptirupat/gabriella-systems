Gabriella Research

Understanding human movement — and the responsibility that follows

Gabriella studies how AI systems can understand human movement while remaining trustworthy to the people they measure.

Research Vision

A longer question behind the product

Gabriella began with a practical question: what can computer vision reliably reveal about athletic movement from ordinary video?

That question leads somewhere deeper.

As AI systems become capable of observing, measuring and personalizing feedback about people across many sessions over time — what happens when the person being modeled is still developing? When the repeated feedback loop of an AI system becomes part of how a young person understands their ability, progress and identity?

Sports gives us a concrete research environment: movement is visible and repeatable, feedback can be structured, and progress is observable. Gabriella's research agenda uses this environment to study questions that may have broader implications for AI systems interacting with developing users across learning, rehabilitation and skill acquisition — though lessons in those domains would require separate future research.

Four Research Pillars

How Gabriella organizes its research agenda

Pillar 1

Visual Intelligence

How can AI reliably understand complex human movement from ordinary video?

  • Human pose and motion understanding
  • Temporal action understanding
  • Player, bat and ball tracking
  • Delivery event detection
  • Camera-view-aware perception
  • Multimodal sports video understanding
Research Area

Pillar 2

Measurement Intelligence

How can visual observations become meaningful, reproducible measurements?

  • Biomechanical measurement from video
  • Uncertainty-aware metrics
  • View-dependent measurement
  • Temporal metrics
  • Movement comparison frameworks
  • Interpretable performance evidence
  • Validity and repeatability of measurements

Not every metric is measurable from every camera view. A core principle of this pillar is that systems should be explicit about what they cannot determine.

Research Area

Pillar 3

Longitudinal Intelligence

How should AI reason about how an individual changes over time?

  • Session-to-session progression
  • Within-athlete baselines
  • Temporal personalization
  • Detecting meaningful change vs. natural variation
  • Avoiding misleading between-athlete comparisons
  • Longitudinal representations of movement

This pillar leads naturally into a fourth question — what happens when the person being modeled is still developing?

Research Area

Pillar 4 — Gabriella's emerging research thesis

Developmental Safety

What changes when an AI system measures, evaluates and gives repeated feedback to a person who is still developing?

A conventional AI safety framework asks whether a system produces harmful outputs. Developmental safety asks an additional question: what happens when AI becomes part of the feedback loop through which a young person understands their ability, progress and identity?

  • Identity and self-perception in AI feedback loops
  • Agency and autonomy in youth-facing AI
  • Responsibility of AI developers to developing users
  • Human authority and oversight in AI-assisted development
Gabriella Research Thesis

Exploratory Research Concept

Provisionally titled

The Algorithmic Mirror

AI systems increasingly do more than answer questions. They observe people, characterize their behavior, personalize responses and provide repeated judgments over time.

For adolescents, that creates a distinct research problem. A system's interpretation can become part of the feedback through which a developing person understands performance, competence and progress.

We refer to this feedback relationship as the Algorithmic Mirror.

This is an exploratory research concept, not a validated psychological or clinical framework. We present it as a lens for identifying research questions.

Research Questions — not conclusions
  • How does repeated AI feedback affect a young person's sense of competence?
  • When does personalization become labeling?
  • Can historical AI assessments constrain how a changing young person is subsequently interpreted?
  • Should developmental AI systems intentionally reduce confidence in old characterizations?
  • How should uncertainty be communicated differently to adolescents than to adults?
  • When should an AI system defer to a coach, educator, parent or other responsible adult?
  • What kinds of comparison motivate improvement, and what kinds may create unhealthy pressure?
  • How should youth-facing AI distinguish measurement from judgment?

These are open research questions requiring interdisciplinary study. No psychological or clinical claims are made here.

Research Question

Research Direction

Developmental Forgetting

"Personalization systems are generally designed to remember. For adolescents, responsible personalization may also require knowing when to forget."

A teenager can change rapidly — in ability, in body, in how they understand themselves. An AI system should not necessarily treat historical characterizations as permanent attributes of a developing person.

We are interested in investigating technical approaches that could account for this, including:

  • Recency-weighted personalization
  • Confidence decay for historical assessments
  • Temporary versus persistent user representations
  • Explicit expiration of developmental labels
  • Uncertainty around longitudinal inference
  • Mechanisms allowing young users to reset or contest AI-generated characterizations
Framing — how AI systems describe observations

"This athlete has poor timing."

Research should instead investigate representations closer to:

"In recent observed sessions under this capture condition, timing differed from the athlete's recent baseline."

The system should describe evidence rather than define the person. This distinction matters more when the person is still developing.

This is a research direction — not a currently implemented Gabriella feature.

Research Hypothesis

Research Theme

Embodied AI and the Developing Self

Most youth-AI research to date has focused on conversational interaction — chatbots, tutoring systems, recommendation engines. Embodied AI introduces a different and less-studied class of interaction.

AI observes the body or behavior → converts observations into measurements → interprets performance → provides feedback → observes the person again → builds a longitudinal model.

This repeated observation-and-feedback loop raises research questions about:

Agency
Self-perception
Identity
Trust
Autonomy
Feedback dependence
Comparison
Human authority

Sports is a useful research environment because repeated movement, feedback and improvement are observable, structured and often explicitly coached. Cricket is Gabriella's initial product domain. Baseball and softball are planned extensions.

Sports is not the only domain in which these questions arise. Lessons from sports AI research could potentially inform systems that evaluate developing users in learning, rehabilitation or skill acquisition — but applying them to those domains would require separate, domain-specific research.

Future Research Direction

Research Principles

Designing AI for Developing Users

These principles reflect the research questions that guide Gabriella's work. They are design aspirations and research orientations, not claims about currently implemented capabilities.

Measurement, not identity

AI should characterize observations — not define the young person. A system should describe what it observed, under what conditions, and with what confidence. It should not permanently categorize a developing person.

Research Principle

Progress over ranking

Where possible, compare an individual against their own longitudinal baseline rather than defaulting to peer ranking. Whether this produces better development outcomes is itself a research question we are interested in studying.

Research Question

Uncertainty should be visible

AI-generated movement measurements and interpretations should communicate their limitations. Not every metric is measurable from every camera view. Not every measurement is equally reliable across all conditions.

Current Product Principle

Human authority matters

AI should support coaches, educators, parents and young people rather than silently replacing consequential human judgment. Research should examine when and how AI systems should explicitly defer to responsible adults.

Research Question

Youth data deserves stronger boundaries

Research should investigate consent, assent, retention, deletion, secondary use and access to video and derived movement and biometric representations of minors. These boundaries differ from those appropriate for adult data.

Future Research Direction

Development changes the model

A model of a young user should account for the fact that the person being modeled is changing — rapidly, and in multiple dimensions. Historical characterizations of a teenager may become misleading within months.

Research Hypothesis

The right to evolve

Historical AI characterizations should not become permanent labels for developing people. We are interested in research into technical mechanisms that give young users some agency over AI-generated representations of them.

Research Context

A growing research need

The questions Gabriella's research agenda addresses are increasingly recognized as important across the AI research community.

OpenAI Teen Development Research Program (September 2026)

OpenAI announced a $5M independent research program studying how generative AI affects young people ages 13–17, highlighting research questions involving identity, agency, self-esteem, emotional development, social connection, age-appropriate AI design, safeguards, individual and contextual variation, privacy and safeguarding.

Source: openai.com/index/teen-development-research-grants/

Note: This program is cited as evidence that these are important emerging research questions, not as evidence of any relationship between Gabriella Systems and OpenAI. Gabriella has not received OpenAI funding, is not an OpenAI partner, and OpenAI has not endorsed Gabriella or its research framework.

Gabriella's research agenda is independently motivated. We study a specific instance of the broader question: what happens when AI systems that are embodied — observing and measuring bodies rather than processing text — interact repeatedly with developing users?

Scientific Advisory

Prof. Mubarak Shah

University of Central Florida

Mubarak Shah is a Distinguished Professor of Computer Science and founding director of the Center for Research in Computer Vision (CRCV) at the University of Central Florida. His research spans computer vision, video analysis, human activity recognition, visual surveillance and machine learning, with decades of contributions to the field.

He is a Fellow of IEEE, a Fellow of AAAI, a Fellow of IAPR, and a Fellow of the ACM. His published work has been widely cited across computer vision and AI research communities, with particular contributions to pose estimation, tracking, action recognition and video understanding.
